Overview

Modeling combustion before committing to hardware.

This project delivered a 2D axisymmetric CFD model of a LOX/LCH4 rocket engine's combustion chamber and converging-diverging nozzle in ANSYS Fluent, used to predict thermal and pressure loads for structural sizing and to characterize nozzle performance ahead of hot-fire testing.

Hot-fire testing a methane engine is expensive and slow to iterate on, so the goal was a CFD model accurate enough to size the regenerative cooling channels and estimate performance before committing to hardware.

Problem

The team needed to predict two things before cutting metal: the chamber wall heat flux profile, which drives cooling channel geometry and material selection, and the nozzle performance (characteristic velocity and thrust coefficient), which determines whether the design would hit its target specific impulse. Neither could be measured directly without a completed engine.

Approach

Because the engine geometry and flow field are rotationally symmetric about the thrust axis, I modeled the chamber and nozzle as a 2D axisymmetric domain, cutting mesh size and solve time dramatically versus a full 3D case while preserving the physics that matter for wall heat flux and nozzle performance.

  • Built the axisymmetric mesh from the injector face through the nozzle exit, with boundary-layer inflation near the chamber and nozzle walls
  • Modeled CH4/O2 combustion with a finite-rate/eddy-dissipation reaction mechanism under the species transport model
  • Used k-omega SST turbulence closure to capture the shear layer downstream of the injector and the nozzle boundary layer
  • Solved with the pressure-based coupled solver, appropriate for the strong density gradients across the combustion zone and nozzle throat
  • Set injector-face boundary conditions from target chamber pressure and O/F mixture ratio, with convective wall conditions to extract local heat flux
  • Ran a mesh refinement study to converge near-wall y+ to approximately 1 for accurate wall heat transfer prediction
  • Cross-checked chamber temperature and C* against 1D thermochemical (NASA CEA) predictions as a sanity check on the reacting-flow setup
The largest source of uncertainty wasn't the turbulence model — it was assuming fully pre-mixed, gaseous propellants at the injector face, which skips over real atomization and mixing.

Results

The model produced a wall heat flux profile that was used directly to size the regenerative cooling channels, and matched bulk combustion parameters from the 1D thermochemical prediction closely enough to trust the CFD-derived nozzle performance numbers.

What I'd do differently

Given more time, I'd replace the pre-mixed gaseous injector assumption with a spray/atomization model for the liquid propellants — that's the biggest gap between this model and how the engine actually behaves at the injector face, and it's where I'd expect the largest correction to the predicted heat flux and mixing efficiency.
